How Medieval Courts Worked:

By Yaoyu Min

Introduction

Although both judicial systems, medieval and modern-day courts differed greatly. Medieval courts had no written laws, leading to inconsistent court rulings. Additionally, the entire structure of the legal system is completely different, and the sentence of the convicted was typically completely abiritary[1]. The predominant courts of medieval Europe —not including ecclesiastical courts — were king’s courts, shire courts, hundred courts, and manorial courts[2]. The following paragraphs will provide an elucidation on every court, as well the trials that medieval courts utilized.

The King’s Court

The curia regis — or king’s court — was a royal council that helped the king in administrative, financial, and legal matters[3]. This passage will be focusing mainly on the latter.

The curia regis was initially a council of noblemen and other men of influence formed to help the king as advisors. However, over time, they became involved in judicial matters[3]. Eventually, the curia regis became so heavily tied with judicial work that they began to establish groups of people specifically to deal with legal matters, exempli gratia the “Court of King’s Bench” in England and the “Parlement” in France[4].

The curia regis tried convicts of serious crimes like murder and treason, as well as nobles/knights who threatened the power of the king[1]. It also punished frequent violators of the law.

Conceptually speaking, the curia regis had the paver and the right to sentence any criminal. However, because the curia regis was, intrinsically, the king’s personal court, they typically did not associate themselves with less serious crimes.

Punishments issued by the king’s court included, but were not limited to: execution, imprisonment, and public humiliation. It could, essentially, give any punishment that was under the church’s dominion[6], examples including excommunication and penance. The king’s court also could not punish anybody within the church’s jurisdiction.

The Shire Court

Because the utilization of shire courts was circumscribed to England[7], we will be covering them less thoroughly than other courts.

Before shire courts were introduced to England, the judicial system of the Anglo-Saxons was less organized and lacked structure. Shire courts were presented as the solution to combat this.

It was the responsibility of the shire courts to resolve issues that the hundred courts could not. As the English law stated, a person could only appeal to a shire court after they had failed to seek justice at a hundred courts three times[8]. Technically, the shire court and the hundred court were on the same bureaucratical level[8,9]. However, the shire court was reserved for more complicated cases. Shire courts resolved minor criminal cases, as well as minor land issues and local disputes. The shire court could return orders, fines, and outlawry.

Hundred Courts

Hundred courts were similar to shire courts in that they were also only located in England. Because of this, we will also be covering them less thoroughly.

Hundred courts dealt with court appeals that were too complicated for manor courts, but too simple for shire courts. Shire courts were established sporadically around England. They dealt with judicial matters and acted as outposts for the English military[10]. However, they eventually shifted away from their militaristic duties and towards judicial functions. The list of punishments that they gave was very similar to that of the shire courts.

Manorial Courts

Unlike the aforementioned two courts, manorial courts were widespread throughout Europe during the feudal period[11]. They were the lowest level of court and were responsible for all legal matters which the lord of the manor had jurisdiction over. There were three types of courts within the manorial court: the court of the honour, the court baron, and the halmote court. Each court was different in that it tried different classes or people — the court of honour tried those close to the lord of the manor, the court baron tried free men/women, and the halmote court tried serfs[11].

Every manor had a list of rules called the custumal. Anybody resident of the manor who broke the rules could be tried in the manorial court, either by trial by ordeal, compurgation, or jury[11]. Punishments included fines and public shaming[12].

Trial By Jury

Trial by jury was one of the most common forms of trials in the middle ages. And saw its emergence in the 13 hundreds[13].

Contrary to popular belief, trial by jury typically did not end in severe punishment. Most of the time, the convicted were either found not guilty or pardoned. Additionally, those who were able to feign literacy could benefit from clergy. This would mean that the convicted would be given the opportunity to go to a church court, where the ruling was typically less harsh[13].

By trial by jury, a panel of juries would listen to testimonies and decide if the convicted was guilty[14].

Trial By Ordeal

Trial by ordeal was a trial in which the accused were thought to be saved by divine intervention if innocent. It is an iconic medieval trial type and is considered incredibly unethical by modern standards.

The main trials that were included with trial by ordeal were trial by fire, hot water, cold water, and combat. Trial by fire and hot water involved forcing the accused to inflict injury on themselves to see if the wound healed fast. Trial by cold water was typically used as a trial to test if one was a witch. In this trial, the accused were thrown into water to see if they floated or sank. If they floated, it would be taken as a sign that the water rejected them, proving their guilt. Finally, in trial by combat, nobles were forced to fight. The winner was innocent and the loser was guilty[15,16].

Trial by Compurgation

Trial by compurgation, despite its relative obscurity, was the most common trial method of the middle ages. It was “looked on as the most effectual proof of innocence.[17]”.

By trial by compurgation, the accused had to swear an oath that they did not commit the crime before finding a specific number of people — typically twelve — and having them swear an oath that they believed the accused’s oath[18]. The twelve people did not have to know anything about the case; trial by compurgation was less a trial of empirical evidence and more a test of character[19].

Conclusion

In the end, medieval justice was less a system than a gamble. A gamble in which power and luck decided more any empirical evidence ever could. Whether a person lived or died could be decided on the mood of a lord, the loyalty of his friends (trial by compurgation), or whether the “heavens” intervened. And while the modern courts may seem to be perfect, they still bear the vestiges of medieval ones. Perhaps we can never get a perfect legal system. But we can get pretty close.
