Punishments and Crimes of Medieval Europe:

By Ien Gordenchuk
Edited By Yaoyu Min

Introduction

The middle ages were a period that marked the rise of civilization in Europe. Although the middle ages were a time when Christianity, feudalism, and cultures flourished all across Europe, there was also a problem with crime. To prevent theft and robberies, authorities used different methods of punishment to keep order in society. Crime was also taken very seriously because many rulers believed that crime was offensive to God and stability.

Punishments

One of the most severe punishments was public executions. People who committed serious crimes such as murder, treason, piracy, or rebellion were often hanged, beheaded, or burnt in public. This was very common because authorities wanted crowds to realize the consequences and reprisals that came with defying authority. Contrary to popular belief, beheading was a rare punishment that was reserved for nobles because it was quicker and more honourable. For ordinary criminals, being hanged was very common. But if someone was accused of heresy - holding religious beliefs that went against the Church— they would be burnt at the stake. In some countries, people who were guilty of treason could face extremely brutal consequences for stealing such as drawing and quartering, which was when someone was dragged to the location of their execution, hanged —but not given the mercy of death — and had some of their organs removed and burnt in front of them before being beheaded and having their body cut up into four pieces, tied to a horse, and pulled apart. Resulting in a painful and merciless death.

For lesser crimes, criminals would receive punishments that caused pain. Whipping or flogging were commonly used when someone committed crimes such as theft, drunkenness or cheating. Criminals were often whipped in public streets so that everyone could see their shame. Stocks or pillory were also common punishments. Stocks were a wooden frame made to restrain the movement of feet and sometimes hands, while the pillory was a standing frame that held the hands and head of the criminal and places in markets or in front of churches for the public to see. People would often throw rotten food and garbage at them. This punishment was rarely used in Italy.

Imprisonment was also very common for all criminals. Unlike modern prisons, medieval prisons were not designed to help improve a criminal's behaviour. Medieval prisons were often used for holding prisoners while they waited for trial, punishment, or execution. Some criminals were also imprisoned if they could not pay their fines for crime.

Imprisonment

Medieval Prisons also had very harsh conditions. A good example of one is Doge’s palace in Venice. Since Venice is built on water, the lower cells of the prison were damp. Prison cells were also often overcrowded, poorly ventilated,and damp,causing disease to spread quickly and easily. Prisoners also did not have access to clean water, proper food, or medicine. Prisoners also had to pay for their own food and bedding if they could afford it. Poor prisoners often suffered the most because they had no money or family support.

Imprisonment could be used for crimes such as theft, fraud, debt, political crimes, or religious offenses. In some cases, important political prisoners or people accused of heresy were kept imprisoned for long periods while authorities investigated them. Torture was sometimes used during imprisonment to force confessions or gather information.

Social class also affected prison conditions. Wealthier prisoners could sometimes pay for better treatment, larger cells, or special privileges, while poor prisoners lived in extremely harsh conditions. Because prisons were so unhealthy and dangerous, imprisonment itself was often considered a serious punishment.

Another example of harsh medieval prisons was the Tower of London. Although the tower was built by William the conqueror around 1077 as a fortress and royal palace, it later became known as a prison where important criminals and political enemies were held. The Tower was mainly used for high-status prisoners such as nobles, religious leaders, political enemies, and people accused of treason. Ordinary criminals were usually kept in smaller local prisons or punished publicly instead. Many prisoners inside the Tower were accused of plotting against the king or challenging royal authority.

Conditions inside the Tower depended heavily on a prisoner’s social class and wealth. Wealthy prisoners could sometimes buy better rooms, furniture, food, and even sometimes servants. Poorer prisoners suffered in cold, dark, damp cells with little sanitation.The Tower was also associated with interrogation and torture, although torture was not used on every prisoner. Some prisoners were stretched on the rack,a device designed to painfully pull apart the body in order to force confessions or information. Torture was usually used for serious political crimes or treason.

Several famous prisoners were held there, including Anne Boleyn and Sir Thomas More, although they were from the later medieval and early Tudor periods. Most prisoners were executed either inside the Tower or nearby on Tower Hill.

Religion

Religion played a major role in medieval justice. Since the Church had great influence and power over society, religious crimes were treated very seriously. People could be punished for criticizing church teachings, spreading banned books, or practicing beliefs considered heretical. During periods of religious conflict, both Catholic and Protestant governments punished people who followed opposing beliefs. Accusations of witchcraft also increased in some regions toward the later Renaissance period.

Punishments Continued

Branding and mutilation were also harsh punishments commonly used during the Middle Ages to permanently mark criminals and scare others away from committing crimes. Medieval governments believed punishment should not only hurt the criminal, but also publicly shame them for the rest of their life.

Branding involved burning a mark or symbol into a person’s skin using a hot iron. The mark often represented the crime they committed. For example, thieves, deserters, or repeat offenders could be branded on the hand, face, or shoulder. Because the scar remained visible forever, everyone could identify the person as a criminal. This made it difficult for former criminals to find work or be accepted by society. Although branding criminals was not used after the middle ages, it was later used to brand individual cows on farms.

Social Class

Social class also often affected punishment: wealthy nobles could occasionally avoid harsh treatment by paying fines or using their influence. Poor people usually faced stricter punishments because they had fewer legal protections. In many places, justice was not equal for everyone.

Conclusion

Despite the harsh punishments presented for committing crimes, the crime rate in medieval Europe was not lower than it is today. The punishments of medieval Europe sought only to punish, not to improve. A system like this was doomed from the start. Medieval punishments show us that cruelty and violence never solve the problem. Mercy and rehabilitation do.
